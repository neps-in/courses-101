# M07.L03 Quiz — SEO Writing That Ranks and Reads Well

> 8 questions · 3 recall, 3 scenario, 2 spot-the-error (Q8 is the spaced review) · pass mark 70% (6 of 8). Q1–Q7 are tagged with the objective they test (O1–O3) so a failed answer points at the exact concept.
> Facts checked 2026-11-09 against GOV.UK's clear-language and clear-structure guidance, digital.gov, Google's SEO Starter Guide, helpful-content page and ranking systems guide, and the Search Quality Rater Guidelines (September 2025).

### Q1. What does GOV.UK suggest for long sentences? *(O1 · recall)*
- a) Keep every sentence under 10 words, whatever the topic
- b) Use semicolons to join short sentences into longer ones
- c) Try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long
- d) Long sentences are fine as long as the keyword is in them

**Answer:** c
**Explanation:** GOV.UK: "Try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long." It also says "Paragraphs should have no more than 5 sentences each."
**Hint:** A number between 20 and 30.

### Q2. Where do the rater guidelines say the most helpful content on a page should go? *(O1 · recall)*
- a) Near the top of the page, so visitors can access it immediately
- b) After a full introduction that sets out the history of the topic
- c) In the footer, where search engines give links the most weight
- d) Spread evenly through the page, so readers have to scroll down

**Answer:** a
**Explanation:** "Web site owners and content creators should place the most helpful and essential MC near the top of the page so that visitors can immediately access it." On recipe pages, "the recipe itself" goes near the top.
**Hint:** Think of a recipe card.

### Q3. What does Google say about readability scores from plugins? *(O3 · recall)*
- a) A green score is required before a post can appear in results
- b) Google uses the Flesch score to rank pages in every language
- c) Posts with a red score are treated as thin and filtered out
- d) Nothing: no Google doc mentions them, so they're a writing aid

**Answer:** d
**Explanation:** No Google doc mentions readability scores. Google asks for content that's "well written, easy to follow", which a formula can only roughly estimate. Use the score to find long sentences, then judge for yourself.
**Hint:** Look for a Google source, and you won't find one.

### Q4. Kavya's post "5 yoga poses for lower back stiffness" starts with a paragraph about the history of yoga. What's the best fix? *(O1 · scenario)*
- a) Keep the history, and add the main keyword to it three more times
- b) Open with what the poses do and the first pose, then the detail
- c) Move the history to a heading called "Introduction" at the top
- d) Add more history so the post reaches 2,000 words in total

**Answer:** b
**Explanation:** Put the most important information first (GOV.UK). The searcher wants the poses, not the history. "Introduction" is a generic heading GOV.UK says to avoid.
**Hint:** What did the searcher ask for?

### Q5. Which sentence shows first-hand evidence? *(O2 · scenario)*
- a) "Cold brew is a popular drink that many people enjoy in summer."
- b) "Experts agree that the grind size matters a great deal for flavour."
- c) "Our 24-hour batch tasted flat, so we settled on 16 hours instead."
- d) "Cold brew is said to be less bitter than other ways of brewing."

**Answer:** c
**Explanation:** It reports a result from Nila's own test, with a number and a change she made. The others could be written by anyone.
**Hint:** Who did something?

### Q6. Amara's draft says "Studies show most readers skim web pages," with a link to a marketing blog. What should she do? *(O2 · scenario)*
- a) Keep it, because any link to a source is enough for Google
- b) Remove the link, because outbound links lose ranking power
- c) Change it to "Everyone knows readers skim", with no link
- d) Find the original study, check it, and cite it by name

**Answer:** d
**Explanation:** Link the primary source, not a blog that repeats it, and name it in the sentence. Google: "make sure you trust the resource you're linking to."
**Hint:** Follow the blog's own link.

### Q7. Spot the error. A writer says: "My post is 900 words but competitors average 2,000, so I'll add a summary, a history section and some general tips to catch up." What's wrong? *(O3 · spot-the-error)*
- a) Nothing; matching the average length is how posts get ranked
- b) The extra sections are filler that makes a page look rich but empty
- c) The summary should go at the top of the post instead of the end
- d) She should add the extra 1,100 words as hidden text in an accordion

**Answer:** b
**Explanation:** Filler "can artificially inflate content, creating a page that appears rich but lacks content website visitors find valuable". Google has no preferred word count. Add first-hand detail where a section is thin, not padding.
**Hint:** Do the new sections help the reader do the task?

### Q8. Spot the error. *(review: m04-l03)* Tomás's drill guide repeats "best cordless drill" in almost every sentence "so Google knows what it's about". What's the problem?
- a) It's keyword stuffing, which tires readers and breaks Google's spam policies
- b) He should repeat it only in the headings, where Google counts every keyword
- c) The phrase should be in the meta keywords tag instead of the post text
- d) Nothing, as long as the post reaches a 2% keyword density in total

**Answer:** a
**Explanation:** "Excessively repeating the same words over and over (even in variations) is tiring for users, and keyword stuffing is against Google's spam policies." Vary the words naturally (Lesson 4.3).
**Hint:** Read it aloud.
